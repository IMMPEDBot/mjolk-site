On fika

A cup, a cookie,
and twenty minutes.

Fika is the most Swedish word that doesn't translate. It means "coffee" — but really, it means stopping. And we think the rest of us have it wrong about what stopping is for.

In Sweden, fika isn't a coffee break — it's a verb, a noun, and a national institution. Twice a day, work pauses, conversation starts, and someone puts a kettle on. There's always something baked. Cinnamon buns, traditionally. Cookies, if you've fallen in love with what we do.

The logic runs almost backwards from how we work in Britain: rest is part of the job. Sweden routinely lands in the top three happiest countries on earth, posts some of Europe's highest worker productivity, and works fewer hours per year than the UK average. Most large Swedish companies write fika into the working day on purpose. It's not a perk — it's infrastructure.

It works because it does three things at once that the British coffee-at-your-desk does none of. It separates work from not-work, even briefly. It turns colleagues into people. And it gives the body something it has wanted since the agricultural revolution: a slow snack and a chair.

i.

Stop. Properly.

Laptop closed. Slack quiet. No "just one more email." Twenty minutes, minimum. Twice a day if you're doing it right.

ii.

Share it.

Fika alone is allowed but it isn't really fika. The point is the person across the table — partner, colleague, your kid after school.

iii.

Eat something sweet.

A bun. A biscuit. A cookie. The food is the excuse for the pause. The pause is the point. The food is also, ideally, very good.

"Vi fikar inte för att producera.
Vi fikar för att vara människor." — roughly: we don't fika to produce. We fika to be human.

Imagine your week with two of these built in every day. The 11am gets a horizon. The 3pm slump gets an answer. Your colleagues become people you actually know. The kid in front of homework eats a warm cookie and tells you what's actually going on at school. Your partner sits down. Nobody's life was ever made worse by twenty minutes, a cup of coffee, and someone they like. We're not saying a tin of cookies fixes anything. We're saying: there's a reason a small Nordic country wrote stopping for cake into its working day, and it's working better than most of what we're doing. Steal it.

If yours has cooled

Bring them back to life.

We send them warm, but if they cool down — or you fancy that fresh-from-the-oven feel the next morning — here's exactly how Mormor would do it.

How to warm them up.

straight from Mormor's notebook

  1. Oven (best): Pop the tin (lid removed) into a preheated oven at 300°F / 150°C for 5–7 minutes, until soft and fragrant.
  2. Microwave (quickest): Take a cookie out of the tin and onto a microwave-safe plate. Heat for 8–12 seconds. Eat immediately.
  3. Storage: Keeps best at room temperature for up to 2 days. For longer, refrigerate. They'll keep a week.
⚠ Never put the metal tin in the microwave. (Sounds obvious. It happens.)
— kram, Mormor x

Need to know

The fine print.

What's fika? +
A Swedish institution. It means "to have coffee" — but really it's a daily pause for coffee, something baked, and a chat. Twice a day if you're doing it right. There's a whole section on this page about it. Our tins are built for it: four cookies, four people, one long fika.
Why is each cookie £30? +
Because we mean it. These are large (≈130g), made in small batches the day they ship, with proper ingredients — single-origin Belgian milk chocolate, 70% Valrhona dark, real pistachio cream and kataifi for the Dubai cookie, two days' rest on every dough. They're priced like a serious gift, because mostly that's what they are. The keepsake tin is included.
Where do you deliver same-day? +
Birmingham only — B-postcodes within roughly 10 miles of our Digbeth kitchen. Order by 11am Mon–Fri and a courier brings your tin between 4 and 7pm. CV, DY, WS and WV postcodes get next-working-day for £5.95 (free over £80). Everywhere else in the UK: Royal Mail Tracked 24, also next-working-day. See full delivery zones →
Are the cookies actually warm when they arrive? +
Honest answer: only on same-day Birmingham, and only if you eat them within an hour of delivery. The tin holds heat reasonably well but cookies cool to room temperature in under an hour. For UK next-day orders the cookies arrive at room temperature; the reheating instructions on this page (3 minutes at 150°C) bring back the gooey middle in one go.
What allergens are in the cookies? +
All four cookies contain wheat, milk, egg and soy. The Dubai Chocolate cookie also contains pistachio (nuts). See full allergens & ingredients →
Can I send a tin as a gift? +
Yes — that's most of what we do. Visit our gift tins page → for occasion-specific bundles, handwritten note options, and recipient delivery.
Do you do subscriptions? +
We do — weekly, fortnightly or monthly, with 10% off every tin and free delivery as standard (any tier). Pause, skip or cancel anytime. See subscription plans →
What if I don't love them? +
Drop us a note within 7 days and we'll either send a replacement tin or refund the order in full. We've used it about a dozen times in two years. We'd rather you came back than feel stuck with a tin you didn't enjoy.
Are the tins reusable? +
Very. Powder-coated steel, food-safe lining, 18cm across, painted Falu red because it's the most Swedish colour we know. Customers use them for biscuits (obviously), tea bags, sewing kits, dog treats, and — in one memorable case — engagement rings. Wash by hand.
